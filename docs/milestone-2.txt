Project Goals
The primary goal of this project is to assess the profound strain that the introduction of artificial intelligence (“AI”) fabricated evidence, specifically highly realistic deepfake videos, could place on the justice system. As discussed in class, previous research establishes a concerning psychological phenomenon: decision-makers often continue to rely on fabricated evidence when rendering a verdict even after they are informed that the evidence’s authenticity is disputed. This project aims to simulate a realistic mock trial scenario to empirically measure this “deepfake dilemma” in action. 

Beyond merely observing this cognitive vulnerability, the broader objective of the project is to evaluate the effectiveness of current procedural safeguards and translate our findings into practical, evidence-based recommendations. Ultimately, the project seeks to guide the legal system on how to better handle potentially fabricated evidence to protect the integrity of judicial outcomes.

Research Questions
In pursuit of the aforementioned goals, our project seeks to answer two research questions:
	•	The Evidentiary Threshold: How much additional trustworthy evidence is required to meaningfully reduce or eliminate a juror’s continued reliance on AI-fabricated evidence once its authenticity has been challenged?
	•	The Efficacy of Procedural Safeguards: Is a standard procedural warning simply instructing the jury that the video’s authenticity is disputed sufficient to correct their perception, or are additional judicial safeguards required?

Why the Form of the Project is Appropriate to Answer the Questions
To effectively answer our research questions, our project simulates a criminal court proceeding in a controlled environment. We are using twenty mock jurors drawn from the general population. This participant pool is appropriate because actual criminal juries consist of ordinary members of the public who typically lack specialized knowledge of artificial intelligence or evidentiary reliability. Furthermore, to ensure we capture candid and authentic judgments, participants will not be informed of the study’s focus on deepfakes as evidence. This approach ensures participants evaluate the evidence naturally. It mimics the experience of real jurors, who enter a courtroom without being primed to look for AI deception.

The step-by-step structure of the simulated trial is also uniquely designed to measure the “deepfake dilemma” in action. Participants will first view a highly realistic, fabricated AI video implicating one of two defendants and will be asked to render an initial verdict along with a confidence rating on a scale of 1 to 10. Crucially, participants will then be informed that the video’s authenticity is contested. Following this, we will introduce additional, increasingly strong non-video evidence in successive layers. After each new layer of evidence is presented, participants must reassess their verdict and confidence rating. By observing when and how participants adjust their decisions as new information is provided, this format allows us to measure the lingering impact of AI-generated evidence once its authenticity is challenged. From here, we can evaluate the amount of additional trustworthy evidence needed to reduce or eliminate jurors’ reliance on the AI-generated evidence, while simultaneously determining whether procedural warnings are sufficient to correct juror perception or if additional judicial safeguards are necessary.

Research & What We Learned:
We conducted research across a variety of sources. These are summarized below:

	•	Maura Grossman & Paul Grimm, “Judicial Approaches to Acknowledged and Unacknowledged AI-Generated Evidence” (2025) 26:2 Colum Sci & Tech L Rev 110: This paper explores the adequacy of current judicial approaches to AI-generated evidence presented in court and highlights the dangers associated with relying on this type of evidence. The authors also flag the “deepfake dilemma,” which explains that once jurors or judges hear or see deepfake evidence, they may struggle to forget that evidence even after its authenticity is called into question.

	•	Paul W Grimm, Maura R Grossman & Gordon V Cormack, “Artificial Intelligence as Evidence” (2021) 19:1 Northwestern J Tech & IP: This paper engages with the adequacy of the existing Federal Rules of Evidence in the United States in addressing concerns posed by AI-generated evidence through the lens of relevance, authentication, and the usefulness of the Daubert Factors. For the purposes of our project, it may be useful to perform a similar analysis that mirrors the one in this paper, but for Ontario instead of the United States. The current proposed rules in Ontario are summarized below:
	•	“Revised Artificial Intelligence Rules Draft 11 (as of Feb. 11, 2026)”: This source introduces procedural rules to govern AI evidence. The rules create a framework for challenging potentially fabricated or altered AI evidence (like deepfakes) and allows judges to exclude such evidence where its prejudicial effect outweighs its probative value. The source is valuable because it illustrates how courts may attempt to regulate AI evidence through disclosure and admissibility standards, which connects directly to our research question.
	•	“Supplementary Town Hall (Virtual) for Revised Draft AI Rules: Minutes”: This source reveals the concerns of judges, lawyers, and policymakers regarding deepfake evidence and the difficulty of balancing transparency, fairness, and practicality. These discussions reinforce the importance of empirical research to determine if warnings about contested video authenticity will be effective in reducing reliance on potentially fabricated AI evidence.

	•	Civil Rules Committee, “Consultation on proposals for Rules of Civil Procedure relating to evidence and Artificial Intelligence: Consultation Paper” (n.d.) online: <https://cdn-res.keymedia.com/cms/files/lt/jess_638935737913212464.pdf>: This source proposes amendments to the Rules of Civil Procedure to address AI evidence. It discusses creating procedures to challenge potentially fabricated or altered electronic evidence and applying traditional expert-evidence reliability standards to AI outputs.
 
	•	Maria‑Paz Sandoval et al., “Threat of deepfakes to the criminal justice system: a systematic review” (2024) 13:41 Crime Science 1: This source explores the severe vulnerabilities courts face regarding AI-generated synthetic media. The review explains that deepfakes make it exceptionally difficult to maintain the integrity of judicial processes because they are highly accessible and easy to use, while detection technologies severely lag behind their development. Furthermore, courts currently lack standardized procedures or rules for addressing AI evidence, leaving judges and lawyers unequipped to properly verify credibility. This creates a critical vulnerability because the justice system has traditionally placed an “inherent” trust in visual media. Because deepfakes can appear entirely real, they can easily facilitate the falsification of evidence and can erode institutional trust. 

	•	Alexander Diel et al., “Human performance in detecting deepfakes: A systematic review and meta-analysis of 56 papers” (2024) 16 Computers in Human Behavior Reports 100538: This source concludes that “human guesses on whether AI-generated content is fake or real are at chance levels.” This finding validates the core premise of our simulated trial: ordinary mock jurors cannot independently identify fake evidence and are highly susceptible to the initial deception. Because humans are so vulnerable to this manipulation, the deepfake effectively becomes reality in the juror’s mind, triggering the “deepfake dilemma.” This vulnerability underscores why our project must measure the amount of additional, reliable evidence required to meaningfully counteract a deepfake’s lingering influence on a verdict.

	•	Klaire Somoray, Dan J Miller & Mary Holmes, “Human Performance in Deepfake Detection: A Systematic Review” (2025) 2025:1 Human Behavior & Emerging Technologies 1833228: This review finds that standard interventions to improve human detection of deepfakes, such as simple warnings, are largely ineffective and can even increase mistrust of authentic videos. This finding supports our second research question, demonstrating why a warning of contested authenticity may fail to stop jurors from relying on contested AI evidence, thereby validating our need to empirically measure the true efficacy of these procedural safeguards. 

	•	Ullrich K H Ecker & Luke M Antonio, “Can you believe it? An investigation into the impact of retraction source credibility on the continued influence effect” (2021) 49 Memory & Cognition 631: This source examines how the perceived trustworthiness of the source issuing a retraction impacts continued reliance on that source (or information), revealing that retractions inherently suffer from a “credibility handicap” because people tend to evaluate the original misinformation as more likely to be true than the correction. This reveals that standard warnings will likely fail to eliminate the subconscious impact of a deepfake.

	•	Klara Austeja Buczel et al., “Vaccination against misinformation: The inoculation technique reduces the continued influence effect” (2022) 17:4 PLoS ONE e0267463: This study finds that the continued influence of misinformation can be eliminated by “inoculating” individuals with counterarguments. This validates our experimental approach by proving that actively introducing new, reliable evidence to serve as a counterargument may be essential to overcoming the “deepfake dilemma.”

	•	Amy J Mickelberg et al., “Did he or didn’t he? Mixed evidence for the continued influence of retracted misinformation on person impressions” (2025) 20:5 PLoS ONE e0322045: This study explains the psychology behind the “deepfake dilemma”: retracting evidence leaves a cognitive “gap” that people will subconsciously fill with the original false narrative. Because an “alternative explanation” is required to successfully fill this gap, it validates our project’s design of introducing new evidence to counteract the jury’s lingering reliance on the deepfake.

Potential Challenges:
	•	Sample Size: Our limited sample size of 20 mock jurors may not be statistically representative of the broader public, which could limit the generalizability of our findings regarding how society at large reacts to AI-generated evidence. 
	•	Potential Bias: Participant responses may be influenced by underlying personal or cognitive biases rather than solely their psychological reliance on the AI evidence. We cannot fully control for these confounding variables given the size and breadth of this project. 
	•	Technology Difficulty: We only have access to a limited amount of software to create our AI-fabricated evidence. It is possible that experts can create far more convincing evidence that may not be flagged by courts as fabricated. This is very concerning and falls outside the scope of our project.
